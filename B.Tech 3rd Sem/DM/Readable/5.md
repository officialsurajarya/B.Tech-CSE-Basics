Chapter 5 explores the **Theory of Logics**, introducing the fundamental concepts of propositions, logical connectives, and predicate logic. Here is a detailed breakdown with definitions and examples.

### **Propositions and Arguments**
A **proposition** is defined as a declarative sentence that is either strictly true or strictly false, but never both. 
*   **Examples of Propositions:** "Delhi is the capital of USA", "5 <= 11", and "Temperature is less than 10 C". 
*   **Examples of Non-Propositions:** Questions or commands like "How are you doing" or "Read this carefully" are not propositions.

Propositions are usually denoted by propositional variables such as $p, q, r,$ and $s$. An **argument** is formed when a set of premises (propositions assumed to be true) yields a conclusion. An argument is considered valid if the conclusion can be logically derived from the premises using established rules of inference.

### **Logical Operators (Connectives)**
Compound propositions are formed by combining single propositions using logical operators. 
*   **Negation ($\neg$)**: Reverses the truth value of a proposition. If $p$ is "Michael’s PC runs Linux", then $\neg p$ is "Michael’s PC does not run Linux".
*   **Conjunction ($\wedge$)**: Represents "p and q". It is true **only** when both $p$ and $q$ are true, and false otherwise.
*   **Disjunction ($\vee$)**: Represents "p or q". It is false **only** when both $p$ and $q$ are false, and true in all other cases.
*   **Implication / Conditional ($p \rightarrow q$)**: Represents "if p, then q", where $p$ is the hypothesis and $q$ is the conclusion. It is false **only** when $p$ is true and $q$ is false. It is logically equivalent to $\neg p \vee q$.
*   **Bi-conditional ($p \leftrightarrow q$)**: Represents "p if and only if q". It is true when both $p$ and $q$ have the exact same truth value. It is logically equivalent to $(p \rightarrow q) \wedge (q \rightarrow p)$.

**Variations of an Implication ($p \rightarrow q$):**
For any conditional statement, you can form:
*   **Converse**: $q \rightarrow p$.
*   **Inverse**: $\neg p \rightarrow \neg q$.
*   **Contrapositive**: $\neg q \rightarrow \neg p$ (This is always logically equivalent to the original implication $p \rightarrow q$).

### **Types of Propositional Statements**
Depending on their truth values, compound propositions are categorized as:
*   **Tautology (Valid)**: A proposition that is always true in every scenario (e.g., $p \vee \neg p$).
*   **Contradiction (Unsatisfiable)**: A proposition that is always false in every scenario (e.g., $p \wedge \neg p$).
*   **Contingency**: A proposition that is neither always true nor always false (e.g., $p \vee q$).
*   **Satisfiable**: A proposition that is not a contradiction, meaning it evaluates to true in at least one scenario.

### **Normal Forms**
*   **Canonical SOP (Sum of Products) / Principle Disjunctive Normal Form**: An expression where every AND term (product term) contains all the variables in either normal or complemented form (e.g., $a'bc + ab'c' + abc$).
*   **Canonical POS (Product of Sums) / Principle Conjunctive Normal Form**: An expression where every OR term (sum term) contains all the variables in either normal or complemented form (e.g., $(a' + b + c) \cdot (a + b' + c') \cdot (a + b + c)$).

### **First-Order Predicate Logic (FOPL)**
Propositional logic sometimes fails to derive meaningful information from general statements. For example, knowing "Every Indian likes cricket" and "Sunny is an Indian" cannot logically yield "Sunny likes cricket" using standard propositional variables alone. FOPL fixes this by introducing **subjects** and **predicates**. 
For example, $NatNo(1)$ means "1 is a natural number", where 1 is the subject and "natural number" is the predicate.

To handle groups without writing endless equations, FOPL uses **Quantifiers**:
*   **Universal Quantifier ($\forall$)**: Asserts that a condition $P(x)$ is true for **all** values of $x$ in the universe of discourse. 
    *   *Example:* $\forall x\ [Indian(x) \rightarrow Cricket(x)]$ means "For all $x$, if $x$ is Indian, then $x$ likes cricket".
*   **Existential Quantifier ($\exists$)**: Asserts that a condition $P(x)$ is true for **at least one** value of $x$. 
    *   *Example:* $\exists x\ [Indian(x) \wedge Samosa(x)]$ means "There exists some $x$ who is both an Indian and likes samosas". Note that the existential quantifier is paired with conjunction ($\wedge$) and not implication ($\rightarrow$).

**Negation of Quantifiers:**
*   $\neg [\forall x\ P(x)]$ is equivalent to $\exists x\ \neg P(x)$.
*   $\neg [\exists x\ P(x)]$ is equivalent to $\forall x\ \neg P(x)$.

### **Examples of Translating Sentences to Predicate Logic**
*   "Not all rainy days are cold": $\exists d\ (Rainy(d) \wedge \neg Cold(d))$.
*   "None of my friends are perfect": $\neg\exists x(F(x) \wedge P(x))$.
*   "Every teacher is liked by some student": $\forall x\ [teacher(x) \rightarrow \exists y\ [student(y) \wedge likes(y, x)]]$.
*   "Some boys in the class are taller than all the girls": $\exists x\ (boy(x) \wedge \forall y\ (girl(y) \rightarrow taller(x, y)))$.
